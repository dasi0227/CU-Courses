My name is Yiwei Wan. For my course project, I reproduced Agentic Context Engineering, or ACE, on the Formula financial reasoning task.

I tested whether a playbook learned from training questions could improve accuracy. I then used JEV to select ten entries for each test question and measured how that affected accuracy and cost.

FTEC5660 · ACE on Formula · Reproducibility

ACE uses one model with three role prompts. The Generator answers each training question using the current playbook. The Reflector compares the answer with the reference and identifies errors. The Generator can use this feedback to retry an incorrect answer up to three times.

The Curator records lessons for later questions. One entry in the learned playbook explains why a quick ratio calculation excludes inventory.

I used DeepSeek V3.1 Terminus. I sampled two training questions and one validation question from each of fifty formula categories. Both sets therefore covered every category.

All three trials used the same two hundred test questions in the same order. I removed the supplied formulas and explanations from the input and scored each numerical answer against the reference.

Group A answered with an empty playbook. Group B started with an empty playbook and updated it over one pass through the training set. Comparing their test scores measures the benefit of the learned entries.

Group C reused the best playbook from the corresponding B trial. This paired comparison measures the effect of filtering the same learned entries.

03 · Validation

Validation Selects the Playbook

FTEC5660 · ACE on Formula · Reproducibility

Every twenty training steps, I evaluated the current playbook on the same fifty validation questions. In trial three, the playbook grew from sixty-five entries at step eighty to seventy-eight at step one hundred. Both versions answered forty-one questions correctly.

I selected the earliest checkpoint with the highest validation accuracy. The orange star marks that checkpoint at step eighty.

04 · Reproduction

Reproducing the Accuracy Gain

+13.67 ppA: 69.17% → B: 82.83%

FTEC5660 · ACE on Formula · Reproducibility

Group B outperformed A in all three trials. Mean test accuracy rose from about sixty-nine to nearly eighty-three percent, or twenty-seven additional correct answers per two hundred questions on average.

The paper reported a gain of eighteen percentage points. I observed a smaller gain of about fourteen points with a different model version and a smaller training set.

FTEC5660 · ACE on Formula · Reproducibility

JEV scored each entry’s relevance to the test question from zero to three. For a quick ratio question, a general reminder about liquidity meets the one-point criterion. An instruction to exclude inventory meets the three-point criterion because it specifies a calculation step.

I selected the ten highest-scoring entries, resolving ties by playbook order. DeepSeek received the selected entries in their original order and produced the answer.

Group C averaged 80.5 percent accuracy, 2.33 percentage points below B. C answered ten fewer questions correctly in the first trial and two fewer in each of the other two.

DeepSeek used about eighty percent fewer tokens with filtering. Scoring the entries added about four and a half million JEV tokens per test.

With JEV scoring, C averaged one second longer per question than B.

I estimated testing cost using DeepSeek’s input and output tokens and JEV’s input tokens. For two hundred questions, the average cost was about eight US cents for A, ninety-nine for B, and thirty-eight for C. Filtering reduced the combined testing cost by about sixty-two percent relative to B.

The model version, prompts, and reflection settings differed from the paper. I would align those settings and repeat the experiment with larger training and validation sets.

To evaluate JEV’s ranking, I would compare its ten entries with ten selected at random or by keyword. Holding the count fixed tests the selection method. I would then vary the count and choose it on the validation set.
